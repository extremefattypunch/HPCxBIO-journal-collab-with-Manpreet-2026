# Review — Submission 193: ThousandWorlds

## TITLE
A carefully built benchmark that fills a real gap; the "deep learning does not yet succeed" claim needs tuning-parity evidence, and the dataset is not reviewable

## SUMMARY

The paper introduces ThousandWorlds, a curated ML-ready benchmark for emulating the climates of potentially habitable exoplanets. It assembles 1,689 GCM simulations of tidally locked waterworlds from five exoplanet GCMs — two designated *target* models (ExoCAM and the UM) evaluated at test time, and three *auxiliary* models contributing training data only — drawn partly from the published literature and partly from bespoke runs placed by a weighted-coverage Sobol design that trades input-space gap-filling against physical plausibility. Each simulation maps eight planet parameters to 53 time-mean atmospheric fields (temperature, humidity, both wind components, cloud fraction, plus surface temperature, OLR and ASR) harmonised onto a common 32x64 T21 grid with 10 relative-isobar pressure levels.

Three nested subsets stage the difficulty: Single-complete (UM only, 48 fields, no missingness), Multi-complete (all five GCMs, 48 fields), and Multi-partial (all five GCMs, 53 fields, with the structured blockwise missingness that arises because different studies used different vertical grids and output different variables). Two evaluation protocols are proposed: a standard protocol reporting area-weighted RMSE and, for probabilistic methods, the energy score; and a *shared-planets* protocol that reports emulator error as a ratio to the disagreement between the two target GCMs on the same planet, placing error on a domain-meaningful scale.

Ten baselines are evaluated (Train-mean, kNN, PCA-GBT, Coord-MLP, Coord-DeepONet, PCA-MLP, ConvDec, SFNO, PPCA-ICM, GPLFR) over five training seeds, with paired test-set bootstrap intervals and two training-composition ablations (removing auxiliary-GCM data, and additionally removing extended-domain data). The two GP-based baselines are strongest — GPLFR best overall, PPCA-ICM second on every subset — and the authors argue the benchmark exposes a low-data, multi-simulator, parameter-to-field regime in which off-the-shelf deep learning does not yet succeed. Under the shared-planets protocol most learned baselines fall below relative RMSE 1, which the authors read as emulators already being useful for target triage and habitability surveys, while noting substantial absolute headroom.

## REVIEW

**Significance.** This is a genuinely useful piece of community infrastructure. The stated barrier — that the raw simulations exist but are "scattered across studies in incompatible formats, on different vertical grids, with different output variables" — is real, and the unglamorous work of harmonising five GCMs onto a common grid with documented provenance is precisely why a benchmark like this does not already exist. The secondary framing is also well chosen: parameter-to-field regression with a handful of inputs, high-dimensional structured outputs, scarce simulator evaluations, and several imperfect simulators is a recurring shape across the sciences that existing scientific-ML benchmarks largely do not cover, since they mostly target data-rich field-to-field prediction. I expect this dataset to be used.

**Originality.** The individual modelling components are standard by design (this is a benchmark, and that is the right choice). The originality sits in two places. First, the *shared-planets* protocol: normalising emulator error by inter-simulator disagreement on the same input converts an uninterpretable RMSE into a decision-relevant quantity, and it generalises well beyond exoclimate to any multi-simulator emulation problem. Second, treating structured missingness as a first-class benchmark challenge rather than an artefact to impute away, with per-method handling documented explicitly (App. B.1). Both are transferable ideas, and I would want a workshop audience to see them.

**Quality.** The execution is above the norm for benchmark papers in several respects. The sampling design (App. A.1) is unusually thoughtful — weighted coverage with a physically motivated density-given-radius and rotation-given-flux prior, plus an honest account of why pure coverage fails (it selects unrealistically dense planets that crash the GCMs) and of the mid-course change from p=2 to p=4. The uncertainty reporting is better than typical: five seeds with standard deviations (Table 11) and, crucially, the recognition in App. F.2 that test-draw uncertainty *dominates* seed uncertainty, quantified with a paired bootstrap that reports the fraction of resamples in which each adjacent ordering holds (Table 14). Reporting "58.0%" for PCA-GBT over PCA-MLP is exactly the right kind of honesty and I want to credit it. The ablations are informative rather than decorative: the finding that auxiliary-GCM data *harms* four of six deep-learning baselines (SFNO -13.8%, Coord-DeepONet -12.8%, Coord-MLP -11.7%) while helping PCA-GBT and GPLFR is a useful research signal, and the authors went further to test whether per-GCM output offsets explain it (they do not), which is more than most ablations attempt.

Two quality concerns are load-bearing, however. The dataset and code are withheld entirely from reviewers (checklist item 5 is answered **[No]**), which for a benchmark submission withholds the central contribution itself. And the per-method hyperparameter search budget is never reported while many selected values sit on grid boundaries — which is the specific evidence a reader needs to accept the paper's headline conclusion about deep learning. Both are detailed below. Neither casts doubt on the dataset's value; the second bears on one claim in the abstract and conclusion.

**Clarity.** Very good. The paper is well organised, the writing is precise, the distinction between "variable" and "field" is introduced early and used consistently, and the appendices are genuinely informative rather than dumping grounds — App. A.2's per-GCM configuration notes, App. B.2's model descriptions (including the observation that a spatially constant input excites only the l=0 mode, so the learned position embedding is SFNO's sole source of spatial symmetry breaking), and App. D.1's physical explanation for why kNN is strong on winds and clouds are all worth reading. Figure 2's missingness visualisation is well designed. A few presentational fixes are listed below.

**Recommendation.** I recommend acceptance. The dataset is a real contribution to an under-served regime, the protocols are thoughtfully designed, and the empirical work is carefully and honestly reported. My reservations are about what reviewers can verify and about how one framing claim is supported, not about the substance of the benchmark — and both are addressable.

## STRENGTHS AND WEAKNESSES

### Strengths

**S1 — Fills a real and specific gap.** Five GCMs harmonised onto a common grid with documented per-simulation provenance, built in collaboration with exoplanet climate scientists, spanning snowball to moist-greenhouse states. The prior art the paper cites makes the gap concrete: the only previous 3D exoclimate emulator was fit pointwise to 60 simulations from a single GCM, and the closest large-dataset precedent had 345 simulations.

**S2 — The shared-planets protocol.** The paper's best methodological idea, and reusable well beyond this domain (see W4 for a caveat on its interpretation).

**S3 — Uncertainty is taken seriously and reported against the paper's own interests.** Both seed and test-draw variability are quantified, the dominant source is correctly identified, and the paired bootstrap in Table 14 openly reports that four methods in the middle of the ranking reverse in 22-42% of resamples.

**S4 — Ablations that produce a research signal.** The negative-transfer result, and the follow-up test ruling out per-GCM offsets as the explanation, point at a concrete open problem and do so with numbers.

**S5 — Structured missingness as a first-class challenge**, with blockwise patterns characterised (Fig. 2: 5.8% of simulation-field pairs unobserved; 826 of 1,117 ExoPlaSim simulations missing the top level; entire-variable absences studywise) and per-method handling spelled out.

**S6 — Careful baseline adaptation.** Adapting a field-to-field architecture (SFNO) to parameter-to-field regression is not trivial, and the reasoning given for the configuration choices — broadcasting parameters as constant channels, disabling spatial normalisation because it would remove the l=0 conditioning component, adding a position embedding — shows real attention rather than default-config box-ticking. Likewise Coord-MLP is a faithful adaptation of the prior pointwise emulator, with the deviations stated.

**S7 — Domain grounding.** The symmetrization argument (App. A.3), the relative-isobar vertical grid, the per-species asinh pivots for trace gases, dividing ASR/OLR by incident flux, and the discussion of regime transitions in App. D.1 all reflect domain judgement that a purely ML-side effort would have missed.

### Weaknesses

**W1 (major, but fixable) — The dataset and code are unavailable to reviewers.** Checklist item 5 is answered [No], on the grounds that the canonical public locations would identify the authors. For a dataset-and-benchmark submission this means the central contribution cannot be assessed at all: format and ML-readiness, metadata quality, licensing, documentation, the per-simulation-to-publication mapping that App. A.2 says lives in the repository, and the `simulation_id` values that App. F.6 asks readers to look up are all unverifiable. Standard remedies preserve anonymity — an anonymised Zenodo or OSF deposit, anonymous.4open.science, or an OpenReview supplementary upload — and I would encourage the authors to provide one (even a representative subset plus the loader and split files) during the discussion period. This is my single largest reservation, and it is entirely procedural.

**W2 (major, and load-bearing for the headline claim) — Tuning parity is undocumented, and many selected hyperparameters sit on grid boundaries.** App. B.3 states the search was manual rather than exhaustive, and App. B.4 refers the reader to "the number of candidate settings evaluated per method (Appendix B.3)" — but B.3 never gives that number, so the per-method search budget is unknown. Meanwhile, in Table 7 the selected value is at the edge of its grid for, among others: Coord-MLP width (1024 of [128...1024]); PCA-MLP hidden width (1024 of [32...1024]) and depth (2 of [1,2]); ConvDec seed channels (2048 of [64...2048]) and weight decay (0.3 of [3e-5...0.3]); SFNO embedding dimension (768 of [64...768]) and weight decay (0.3); Coord-DeepONet trunk width (512 of [64...512]), depth (3 of [2,3]) and learning rate (0.003, the grid maximum). Boundary selection also affects the GP winners — GPLFR latent dimensionality (150 of [50,100,150]), inverse-temperature (0.1 of [0.01,0.03,0.1]) and global learning rate (0.3 of [0.03...0.3]); PPCA-ICM number of PCs (150 of [20...150]).

Because the abstract and conclusion claim that "off-the-shelf deep learning does not yet succeed" in this regime, a reader needs to be able to rule out the alternative explanation that the deep-learning baselines were capacity- or regularisation-limited by a truncated search rather than by the data regime. Concretely I would ask for: (a) the number of settings actually evaluated per method, and how the manual search was terminated; (b) grids extended along the capacity and regularisation axes until the selected value is interior for every method; (c) total tuning compute per method, alongside the per-run times already in Table 8. Note that this does not touch the dataset contribution — only the framing of one claim. It would also be worth engaging McGreivy and Hakim (2024), *Weak baselines and reporting biases lead to overoptimism in machine learning for fluid-related partial differential equations*, which is squarely on point for a benchmark making a cross-family claim and is currently uncited.

**W3 (moderate) — The two variance sources are quantified but never combined, and the two orderings disagree.** Seed variability is in Table 11, test-draw variability in Tables 13-14, and App. F.2 correctly notes the latter dominates — but the paired bootstrap is run on seed 0 only, so no reported interval covers both sources. The consequence is visible in the paper: Table 1's five-seed geometric means order the middle group PCA-MLP (0.546) < ConvDec (0.547) < PCA-GBT (0.550) < SFNO (0.551), whereas Table 14's seed-0 adjacency chain ranks ConvDec > PCA-GBT > PCA-MLP > SFNO. Both are defensible and the text does say these methods are closely matched, but as written a reader cannot extract either a single defensible ordering or a single interval for this group. Suggestion: resample test examples while also resampling (or averaging over) seeds, report one combined interval per method, and state plainly that the middle four are statistically indistinguishable rather than presenting an ordering for them.

**W4 (moderate, interpretive) — The shared-planets comparison is asymmetric in a way that deserves explicit discussion.** The emulator conditions on the GCM label and is trained on other simulations from the same target GCM, so it has had the opportunity to learn that GCM's systematic biases; the competing GCM has not. A relative RMSE below 1 therefore does not establish that the emulator is as scientifically trustworthy as a GCM — in part it measures how much target-GCM-specific structure the emulator has absorbed. The test planets are held out, so this is legitimately learned structure rather than leakage, and Sec. 3.2 does hedge appropriately ("for tasks where predictive accuracy matters more than physical consistency or interpretability") while Fig. 3 shows the tails. But the conclusion's phrasing — "the best baselines emulate individual GCM outputs more closely than GCMs agree with each other" — will be read as a fidelity claim. A short paragraph making the asymmetry explicit would strengthen the contribution rather than weaken it. Relatedly, with 64 and 62 shared planets and one GCM per lineage, the denominator is itself a noisy two-model estimate of inter-GCM spread, as App. E acknowledges.

**W5 (minor, should be fixed) — Arithmetic error in the Table 4 caption.** It states that the 1,343 training-only simulations "comprise 48 simulations from target GCMs that are outside the core domain (Table 3) and 1,395 simulations from auxiliary GCMs". The auxiliary counts in the table body sum to 159 + 19 + 1,117 = 1,295, and 48 + 1,295 = 1,343; App. E independently writes "1,117 of 1,295 simulations". So **1,395 should read 1,295**.

**W6 (minor) — Subset sizes deserve a line in the main text.** The test sets are 128 / 121 / 74 simulations (standard) and 64 / 62 (shared-planets), and Single-complete trains on only 203 simulations. App. E is appropriately cautious about this, but readers will over-interpret the Single-complete column of Table 1 unless its size appears where the results do.

**W7 (minor) — The strongest baseline is the least independently established.** GPLFR is introduced in a concurrent 2026 reference and is described comparatively briefly (App. B.2) relative to the weight it carries in the conclusion. A short statement of how its objective differs from PPCA-ICM's, together with the per-method tuning budgets requested in W2, would help the benchmark read as neutral infrastructure rather than as a venue where one recent method wins.

**W8 (presentation).**
- Typo: "likley" (App. E, GCMs paragraph).
- The submitted PDF renders visible coloured boxes around every citation and cross-reference, which is distracting in the dense tables — consider `hidelinks` or `colorlinks=false`.
- Figures 4-6 are very dense; one guiding sentence per figure naming what the reader should look at would help a non-specialist ML audience.
- Table 1's caption says geometric means are reported relative to Train-mean's, but the Multi-partial row shows Train-mean as 1.00 and the other two subsets as 1.000 — harmonise the precision.

### Questions for the authors

1. How many hyperparameter settings were actually evaluated per method, and how was the manual search terminated? (W2)
2. If the capacity and weight-decay grids are extended until no selection is on a boundary, does the GP-versus-deep-learning gap on Multi-partial persist?
3. Could an anonymised copy of the dataset — or a representative subset with the loader and split files — be made available during the discussion period? (W1)
4. For the shared-planets protocol, it would be informative to see relative RMSE for a variant trained without any simulations from the GCM being treated as ground truth in each pair. That would separate "reproduces this GCM's idiosyncrasies" from "captures the shared physics", and speaks directly to the scientific-utility reading. (W4)

## LIMITATIONS FIELD

Largely yes. App. E and the checklist cover the restriction to tidally locked waterworlds and the exclusion of dry planets, eccentric orbits and asynchronous rotators; the auxiliary set's dominance by an intermediate-complexity GCM; the shared heritage among the four high-fidelity GCMs (UM/LFRic, ExoCAM/ExoCAM-pre-2022) and what that means for the inter-GCM spread estimate; and the small test sets. The broader-impact discussion correctly identifies emulator overconfidence propagating GCM biases into scientific conclusions as the main hazard. The paper is commendably direct throughout.

Two additions would make the limitations complete: (i) the asymmetry in the shared-planets protocol described in W4 above, since the current phrasing invites a stronger fidelity reading than the protocol supports; and (ii) an explicit statement that the per-method hyperparameter search was manual and of undocumented size (App. B.3), because the "off-the-shelf deep learning does not yet succeed" conclusion rests on it. No negative societal-impact concerns beyond the overconfidence issue the authors already raise.
