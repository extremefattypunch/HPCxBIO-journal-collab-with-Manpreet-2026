# Review — Submission 274: The Solver Is a Labeler

## TITLE
A valuable framing of solver fallback as selective feedback, reported with exemplary honesty; the method is under-specified and the parameter governing its central tradeoff is never swept

## SUMMARY

The paper observes that when a neural PDE surrogate is paired with a numerical solver invoked whenever a gate judges the surrogate unreliable, the solver plays two roles at once: it corrects the current output, and it reveals a label about surrogate error. Because the router decides when the solver runs, those labels are missing according to the current policy rather than at random — so a gate updated only on routed cases estimates risk for the routed tail rather than for the operating stream, and can remain confident on a shifted population precisely because it never queries there.

The authors formalise solver calls as logged selective feedback with recorded action propensities, and evaluate a minimal policy (SENTINEL-IPW) that reserves a few calls per block for a uniform random sample — "sentinels" — allocates the remainder to the highest gate scores, and updates a logistic gate on returned losses using inverse-propensity weights with a floor and a clip. Block risk is estimated from the sentinel subset, which is design-unbiased.

The audit is offline and controlled, which is the point: complete losses are computed but revealed to the policy only when it pays for them. Two 1D PDE families are used — periodic advection-diffusion with an analytic reference, and viscous Burgers with a conservative Rusanov reference — with a frozen 96-feature extreme-learning-machine surrogate, chronological shift stages, 20 prespecified seeds, and a solver budget held at exactly 15% for all four compared policies (uniform random, static learned routing, naive online routing, and SENTINEL-IPW).

The findings deliberately split. On advection-diffusion, sentinel feedback lowers mean returned loss by 8.3% and 95th-percentile loss by 11.5% relative to naive online updating. On Burgers it cuts block-risk-estimation error by 41.0% but raises mean deployed loss by 7.4%, with the static router better still — because three exploratory calls displace cases a useful static score would have caught. The authors present this as an audit and a boundary condition rather than a method win, concluding that when fallback labels are policy-selected, reliability claims require a sampling design as well as a gate, and offering a concrete per-call logging schema for deployed hybrid systems.

## REVIEW

**Significance and originality.** The framing is the contribution, and it is a good one. "The fallback is also the measurement instrument, and it samples according to the policy" is a real and under-discussed property of hybrid surrogate-solver deployments, and importing the logged-bandit and selective-labels formalism — propensity logging, positivity, Horvitz-Thompson estimation — into neural-PDE reliability is the right move. Sec. 5's observation that a single solver call simultaneously serves three competing purposes (correct the present output, measure current risk, improve future routing), that those purposes contend for one budget, and that a score-optimal call is generally not a probability sample, is the sentence I would most want a workshop audience to take away. The technical machinery itself is entirely standard; the originality is in the transposition and in recognising that the resulting benchmark unit is a chronological decision log rather than a fixed test set.

**Quality.** The experimental hygiene matches the claim being made. The comparison is at exactly matched budget, separately verified (Fig. 5); seeds are prespecified; the tolerance was fixed before the final aggregate run; the design is paired across policies within seed; per-seed distributions are reported rather than aggregates alone. For a comparative claim about what is essentially a generic wrapper, matched budget plus pairing is exactly what is required, and many papers making similar claims do neither.

The reproducibility is a model for workshop submissions: complete harness, fixed seeds 0-19, raw per-seed CSV, JSON summary, figure scripts, a machine-readable evidence manifest with a SHA-256 digest, and a guard script that checks every number transcribed into the paper against that manifest — the whole thing CPU-only in under a minute. App. L even discloses a preliminary unstable-timestep bug and states that no numbers from the failed run were retained.

Against that, two substantive gaps. The weighting scheme is under-specified in a way that matters for what the headline number means (W1), and the single parameter that governs the tradeoff the paper is about is never varied (W2). A third, more conceptual issue is that neither experiment demonstrates the causal chain the paper argues for (W3).

**Clarity.** The prose is clear, economical and admirably direct — the paper repeatedly states what its results do *not* show, in the abstract as well as the limitations section. The presentation has real defects, though: one figure is illegible as rendered, another is duplicated between main text and appendix, and the main text spends a float on the metric the paper itself argues is uninformative while the most informative evidence (the paired per-seed comparisons) sits in the appendix. These are all cheap to fix. The citation practice (W8) is a separate and more serious presentational problem.

**Recommendation.** Borderline accept. The framing, the honesty and the logging schema are worth putting in front of this workshop's audience, and a well-executed boundary-condition result is a legitimate contribution. What holds me at borderline rather than clear accept is that the positive claim is thin — one system, one untuned operating point, no frontier — and that the method as described is not the method named. Both are addressable, and the sentinel-share sweep in particular is cheap given the harness runs in under a minute; that single addition would move my score up.

## STRENGTHS AND WEAKNESSES

### Strengths

**S1 — The central framing is genuinely useful and transferable.** See above. The distinction the paper draws between routed-label accuracy and stream calibration, and its insistence that Eq. (1) "protects identifiability, not dominance", are both clean and correct.

**S2 — Unusual and commendable honesty.** The adverse Burgers result is in the abstract, not buried. Sec. 4 states plainly that the tolerance-violation metric is uninformative because it saturates above 0.81. App. F concedes that *uniform random routing* — not the proposed method — is the strongest risk-measurement baseline, because all 15 of its calls are probability samples. App. G reports per-seed heterogeneity instead of pooling the two systems into one favourable average. App. H pre-commits to dropping the architectural component if residual thresholding matches the learned gate at equal wall time. This is how a boundary-condition paper should be written.

**S3 — Matched-budget, paired, prespecified experimental design.** As described above. The exact-budget check in Fig. 5 is the right instinct even if the figure itself is redundant.

**S4 — Exemplary reproducibility**, including the evidence manifest, the digest, the guard script and the disclosed debugging failure.

**S5 — The logging schema (App. I) is the most directly actionable output.** Recording selection probability, sentinel indicator, model/gate/solver versions, input provenance, returned quantity of interest and wall time per call is concrete advice a practitioner can adopt tomorrow, independently of whether SENTINEL-IPW is the right policy. The paper is right that periodic random calls are better understood as a measurement channel than as a heuristic baseline.

**S6 — Correct scoping of what a solver label is.** Sec. 5's treatment of "label" as explicitly solver-relative — a converged numerical answer can label surrogate error with respect to the *implemented* PDE but cannot certify omitted physics — is the right epistemic care, and App. J's warning that the fallback solver may share structural error with the labels it generates is well taken.

### Weaknesses

**W1 (major, technical) — As specified, the weighting is not inverse-propensity weighting: the clip binds on every sentinel in every block.** With 15 solver calls per 100-case block and three uniform sentinels (Sec. 3), a sentinel's inclusion probability is 3/100 = 0.03, so its inverse-propensity weight is 33.3 — which exceeds the stated clip of 20 (Sec. 2, App. C). Score-based calls are chosen by a deterministic top-(k - k_s) rule and so carry propensity approximately 1. The gate update is therefore a fixed 20:1 reweighting of sentinels against routed cases in every block, with sentinels systematically receiving about 60% of their design weight. That is not an inverse-propensity scheme, and it is not a configuration-dependent bias-variance tradeoff — it is a constant, undisclosed misweighting.

Sec. 2 explicitly promises to "report the induced bias-variance tradeoff rather than treating clipping as innocuous", but no such report appears anywhere: there is no sensitivity to the clip value, no distribution of realised weights, no statement of how often clipping binds, and no numeric value is ever given for the floor p_min (it appears only symbolically, in Algorithm 1 and Eq. 1). Since the reported SENTINEL-IPW routing losses are produced by this weighting, the advection-diffusion headline is a result about one undocumented weighting rather than about IPW.

Requests: (a) state p_min; (b) report the realised weight distribution and how often the clip binds; (c) show sensitivity to the clip value; and (d) either rename the policy, or make the score-based selection stochastic (soft top-k or epsilon-greedy over scores) so that positivity holds by construction and the weights are genuinely inverse-propensity. Note that this does not undermine the *audit* — the block estimate is the sentinel mean, which is design-unbiased regardless of clipping — but it does mean the method's characterisation and its routing numbers need repair.

**W2 (major, and the cheapest fix) — The parameter governing the entire tradeoff is never varied.** The thesis is that measurement and control compete for a fixed budget, and the sentinel share k_s/k is precisely the knob that trades them. It is fixed at 3/15 throughout, and App. H lists sweeping it "from 0 to the full budget" as work required before deployment claims. Without a sweep there is no evidence that 3 is a sensible operating point, no located crossover, and — most importantly — no frontier, so the two experiments read as two anecdotes with opposite signs rather than as two points on a curve. The paper's own closing recommendation, that "a policy should therefore choose its sentinel share from the value of information across future blocks", is exactly what the missing sweep would inform. Given that the whole harness runs in under a minute on CPU (App. L), even a coarse sweep over k_s in {0, 1, 3, 7, 15} on both families would be inexpensive and would convert the central claim from qualitative to quantitative. This is the single change that would most raise my score.

**W3 (major, conceptual) — Neither experiment demonstrates the mechanism the paper argues for, and the headline number is reported unpaired.** The stated claim concerns identifiability: stream risk is not recoverable from routed cases without a known nonzero propensity. Burgers supports that half (risk-estimation MAE 0.352 to 0.208) but routing loss worsens. Advection-diffusion supports the routing half (mean loss 0.486 to 0.446) but there the method's risk-estimation MAE gets substantially *worse*, 0.033 to 0.053 — roughly 60% worse than naive, as App. F explains, because only three of fifteen calls are probability samples. So in neither system does the chain "better measurement to better gate to better routing" run end to end, and the mechanism offered for the advection-diffusion win in Sec. 4 ("sentinel observations help the gate move solver effort toward more damaging errors") is a control-side story the identifiability framing does not predict.

A plausible alternative reading is that advection-diffusion is simply a regime with little selection bias — 82% of shifted cases exceed tolerance, so the routed tail closely resembles the stream — in which case sentinels can only cost measurement precision there, which is exactly what Table 1 shows. The paper comes close to saying this ("better measurement and better immediate control are distinct objectives") but should state directly that the 8.3% headline is not evidence for its identifiability thesis, and ideally exhibit a regime where both objectives improve together.

Separately, that 8.3% is quoted from unpaired aggregates whose intervals overlap substantially (0.486 +/- 0.019 versus 0.446 +/- 0.016). A paired 20-seed design is available and Fig. 11 plots the paired differences, but no paired statistic is ever reported; Fig. 6's caption says "consistently favorable" without a number. The paired mean difference with an interval (or a sign/Wilcoxon test) is both the right statistic here and much tighter, and it should be what the abstract quotes.

**W4 (moderate) — The gate observes the shift variables directly, which makes routing easier than deployment and may explain the strong static baseline.** Gate features are the standardised PDE parameters plus one-sided distances beyond the training support (App. C), while the chronological stages are defined by moving exactly those parameters — c, k and nu for advection-diffusion; amplitude, viscosity and wave number for Burgers. The support-distance features are therefore close to an oracle shift detector, and in deployment the generating parameters are often not available as clean features.

This bears directly on the paper's most interesting negative result: on Burgers the *static* router attains the lowest mean loss of all four policies (0.281), which is what one would expect if the parameters alone already carry most of the signal and online updating has little to add. Since the paper's premise is that online updating is where the endogenous-label hazard lives, a setting in which online updating turns out to be unnecessary weakens the demonstration. I would like to see the audit repeated with the raw shift parameters withheld from the gate — output-derived features only (energy, roughness) — which is both more realistic and a sharper test.

**W5 (moderate, scope) — The surrogate's error structure may not be representative.** App. C is admirably clear that the 96-feature ELM with ridge readout is a deliberate demonstrator, not a competitor to FNO or DeepONet, chosen for being fast, deterministic and fully reconstructible. The concern is not competitiveness but transfer: a frozen random-feature linear readout degrades smoothly and close to monotonically in the input parameters, so its error is unusually *predictable* from exactly the features the gate observes. Deep operator surrogates fail in more spectrally and spatially structured ways, and the magnitude of the selection bias being audited is a property of that error structure. Sec. 6 lists dimensionality, simulated data, reference solver, architecture and tuning as limits but does not separate "the operator is lightweight" from "the operator's error structure may make selection bias atypically small or atypically easy to detect". That deserves a sentence, because it conditions how far these numbers travel.

**W6 (minor) — Eq. (1) is not the estimator used.** Sec. 2 presents the Horvitz-Thompson form and then immediately notes that in the fixed-block implementation the design-unbiased block estimate "is simply the mean loss on the uniform sentinel subset". That is a perfectly good choice, but it leaves Eq. (1) decorative. Either use it — which requires stochastic scoring, per W1 — or present the sentinel-mean estimator as the actual estimator and keep Eq. (1) explicitly as motivation.

**W7 (presentation).**
- **Figure 4 is broken in the rendered PDF**: the stage labels overflow their boxes and overlap the connecting arrows, so the pipeline is illegible ("Nominal PDE draws" runs into "Frozen ELM operator", and so on).
- **Figure 3 and Figure 12 are the same table**, presenting identical tolerance-violation rates (adv 0.821/0.819/0.818/0.818; burg 0.510/0.459/0.457/0.468). One should go — and since the paper itself argues this metric is saturated and uninformative, the main-text float would be far better spent promoting the paired per-seed comparisons (Figs. 10-11), which are the strongest evidence in the paper and are currently buried in the appendix. Both are tables presented as figures; consider making them tables.
- In Figure 5 the "adv" series is entirely hidden beneath "burg", so the figure cannot show what its caption claims for both experiments; a small offset or two panels would fix it. A single sentence would arguably serve better than the figure.
- The abstract's "20 chronological streams in two controlled PDE families" reads as 20 streams in total; it is 20 seeds per family.
- The text's "7.4%" for the Burgers mean-loss increase computes to 7.2% from Table 1's displayed 0.291 and 0.312 — presumably rounding of the displayed values, but given the guard script checks transcribed numbers against the manifest, one more significant digit (or the paired difference per W3) would remove the ambiguity.
- NeurIPS checklist item 2 attributes the limitations discussion to "Section 5", but limitations are Sec. 6; Sec. 5 is related work.

**W8 (moderate, scholarly presentation) — The citation practice will cost the paper credibility with exactly the readers it should persuade.** The closing paragraph of Sec. 6 attaches 29 references to a single broad sentence spanning simulation-based inference, weather, materials and digital twins, and Sec. 1 attaches 11 to another. A bracket of 29 conveys nothing about which work bears on which claim. More consequentially, App. K states that "the bibliography was assembled from the supplied full-text corpus and its identity-resolved manifest" and advises that "recent related preprints should be rechecked immediately before any later archival submission" — phrasing that reads as a description of an automated assembly process rather than of engagement with the literature, and which invites exactly the wrong inference about a paper whose actual scientific content is careful, internally consistent and self-critical. I would cut App. K entirely, prune the bibliography to work that is load-bearing, and cite each remaining item at the specific claim it supports. Also, reference [50] (Zhang et al., "Physics-based models outperform AI weather forecasts", 2026) carries no venue or identifier.

### Questions for the authors

1. What is p_min, how often does the weight clip bind, and how do the results move if the clip is set to 5, 20 or 100? (W1)
2. Can you provide even a coarse sentinel-share sweep on both families — say k_s in {0, 1, 3, 7, 15}? (W2)
3. What is the paired per-seed mean difference, with an interval, for SENTINEL-IPW versus naive online routing on advection-diffusion? (W3)
4. Does the advection-diffusion routing benefit survive if the gate sees only output-derived features rather than the true shift parameters? (W4)
5. Is there a regime in these two families where sentinel feedback improves risk estimation *and* routing loss simultaneously? If not, is the honest conclusion that the sentinel share should be tuned per system rather than that sentinels help?

## LIMITATIONS FIELD

Yes, and unusually well. Sec. 6 is direct about the 1D, simulator-only, noise-free setting; the deliberately lightweight operator; the solver-relative rather than physical reference; the untuned gate architecture, block length, tolerance and sentinel share; the use of normal intervals in place of distribution-free uncertainty; and — creditably — the fact that the chosen allocation can actively harm the control objective. App. J covers the false-reassurance hazard and the risk that the fallback solver shares structural error with the labels it generated, and correctly insists that solver-relative monitoring must not be described as physical validation. App. B is honest that the reference discretisation is not a continuum-accuracy claim and that the audit should be repeated at 128 and 256 cells against a spectral or implicit implementation.

Three additions would complete it: (i) the clipping and propensity specification issue in W1, which is currently an unfulfilled promise in Sec. 2 rather than a stated limitation; (ii) error-structure representativeness of the ELM surrogate (W5), which Sec. 6 does not distinguish from "the operator is lightweight"; and (iii) the gate's access to the true shift parameters (W4). One cross-reference fix: checklist item 2 points to Sec. 5 for the limitations, which is related work — it should be Sec. 6.

No societal-impact concerns; the broader-impacts discussion is proportionate to the work.
